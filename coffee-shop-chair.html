Coffee Shop Chair A woman named Abina sat at her bistro table, her book held up in front of her face, and a green tea resting on the table. A man named Will had his back to her. Across the coffee shop, a couple used a full range of positive expressions. They had definitely just met. Abina and Will had not… yet. Will shifted his oatmeal cookie to his other hand as he reached back, feeling for the edge of a chair before pulling it out. As he bent, he froze, almost offended to see Abina, as if she dared to be at the table already. He gave her a glance of quick disinterest; her earthy green headband, natural hair, and strapless boho dress registering only as blurred shapes and colors. “Oh. Anyone sitting here?” he asked. “Yeah,” Abina said. “You.” He was disciplined when it came to weightlifting. That was apparent even under his loose Wu-Tang shirt. The sweatpants were another giveaway. The thick black rimmed glasses showed his age though. He wasn’t wearing contacts. It was like he was starting to accept his diminished youth. He probably grew up in an era when oatmeal cookies were considered health food. He forced a polite smile, sat, and turned away from her all at the same time. He brought the pastry to his mouth and nibbled. He was hyper-focused, watching the couple as if they were well-known actors in a scene. Abina’s eyes ping-ponged back and forth from her book to the new stranger. She finally tilted her head and asked, “You don’t like that cookie, do you?” Will slowly turned and lowered his head, keeping his eyes on the couple until the last second. Then he shot a rapid glance at his phone and back to the couple. “11:48,” he said. Abina laughed. “Should I get ready to call the cops?” she asked. “Cops?” he said, repeating the last word. “Well,” she said, “you’ve obviously been Youtubing ‘how to skin a deer’—planning to kill two innocent people and wear their skin as a Halloween costume.” That got his attention. He finally turned, his face shifting to amused shock. “Don’t worry,” she said, lifting her green tea for a sip. “I won’t tell anybody– as long as you don’t kill the witness.” He let out a soft laugh and put his head down before looking at her again. “I look like a serial killer to you?” he said. “Lemmee see,” she said, leaning forward, “turn your head.” Will played along and looked over his shoulder. Abina sat back with mock skepticism. “Hm,” she said, “I guess not. But just to be sure.” Abina extended the tiniest of digits. “Pinky swear you won’t kill me.” He looked at her finger, hesitating as his mind caught up to the consistency of her wit. Then he wrapped his pinky around hers. “Pinky swear,” he chuckled. He shook his head and looked at the couple. “Really?” Abina said. “You’re really not gonna tell me?” “Tell you what?” “Why you’re so infatuated with those people.” “I’m not infatuated,” Will said, “I just–they're just at my favorite table.” “Oh,” Abina said, leaning back. “You must be one of those military types who has to face all the exits, huh?” “I look like a military type?” he asked. “No,” she said, “You look like you have a secret. Like there’s a reason you wanna sit over there, but you don’t wanna say.” Will turned his head with the guilt of someone not expecting to be caught. Abina closed her book. “Oh, this is juicy. You’re embarrassed.” “Embarrassed?” he echoed. “Yeah. You’re embarrassed. Or—” her eyes widened with mock excitement, “you can’t tell me because… you’re covert ops and the barista’s a terrorist?!” Abina tossed a dramatic glance at the employee, hair jiggling and stopping quickly. Will laughed. “That’s not it.” “Sure,” Abina said, winking with the exaggerated flair of a cartoon character. “You wouldn’t believe me. And it is embarrassing. It’s just not something I would tell a girl– a woman– a young lady.” “So. It's something sexist? I mean, more sexist than what you just said?” “No, it’s not sexist. It’s just–” Abina raised her eyebrows and waited. “Okay,” Will replied, giving in. “You see the guy, right?” “The guy that’s part of the couple that we’ve been talking about this whole time? Yeah. I see him.” “The chair he’s sitting in… it’s magic. Every time I sit there, I meet someone.” “Okay. So it’s like your lucky chair?” “I guess. Kinda. It was like a year ago. I stopped in to see if they had breakfast. They didn’t, so I ordered this gross oatmeal cookie and sat right over there. A little while later, somebody sat in the chair next to me, and we started talking. Afterwards, we kicked it. I forgot all about it until a few months ago when I stopped in again, and the same exact thing happened. I still didn’t put it together. It wasn’t until last week, walking by, that I thought, ‘Okay, if I go in there and meet someone, that means there’s really something to it.’ I come in, I sit, somebody else sits, and we start talking. And now—I’ve come here the last four days, sat in that chair, and met someone every time.” “It’s a coffee shop. You meet people in coffee shops. You’re meeting me right now.” “No. It’s different. Every single time, I meet a woman… and we go to my house immediately and have—” Will leaned in and mouthed the word, “Sex.” “Oh,” she said. “Okay.” “It sounds crazy, but—look at those two,” he said, nodding to the couple, “They obviously just met—” “Obviously.” “—and I bet they’ll leave together.” Abina looked as if she might disagree until, across the cafe, the woman took a sip of her hot coffee and put it back down on the table quickly. She snatched away and rubbed her fingers together. The man got up and grabbed a cup sleeve from the counter. He handed it to her. “Okay, maybe,” Abina said, “But that doesn’t mean the chair is magic. You probably just got lucky because you were ready to get lucky.” “What?” “You think the chair is magic, so every time you sit, you're convinced you know what’s gonna happen, and you project an air of confidence. And that self-assured attitude makes you seem more comfortable in your skin—and thus more attractive.” “Don’t ‘thus’ me,” Will said. Abina laughed. “I’m just saying.” “Maybe,” Will said, looking over his shoulder. “Okay then,” Abina said, “What’s the bet?” “What bet?” “The bet. You said you bet they’ll leave together. How much? Five hundred bucks?” “You got five hundred bucks in cash?” “No.” “I was about to say.” “You wasn’t about to say shit.” Will looked down, like he was trying to see through the table to make out Abina’s figure. He bit his lip. “Okay,” he said. “If I win—” “Ew,” Abina cut him off. “Never mind.” “What?” “The way you just looked at me. It was gross. I don’t wanna bet anymore.” “Alright,” he shrugged, and turned away. “And I was just thinking you didn’t seem like the ‘love ‘em and leave ‘em’ type,” she said. “But then you get all creepy.” “Why am I creepy?” “Because of the way you looked at me.” “How did I look at you?” “I don’t know. Like I was a steak.” “What’s wrong with steak?” “Nothing. But I’m not a steak. I don’t want you to chew me up and shit me out when you’ve had your fill.” “Nice. You should be a rapper.” Will turned back to the couple and then immediately looked away from them. He took the tiniest bite of his cookie. Then, he looked up and out the glass doors. Abina stared at him the whole time. After some silence, they both spoke. “So what? Now you’re not gonna–” “What about you–?” Then they both said “What?” at the same time. “Go ahead,” Abina said, “What was you about to say?” “I was just gonna ask if you came here often,” he said. “I do. I live right down the street.” “You come here to read?” “Yup.” “Hm.” “‘Hm’ what?” Will shrugged and hummed a cadence that met all the inflections of “I don’t know.” “It’s just that if I tried to read here, I wouldn’t be able to concentrate,” he said. “There’s too much going on—the machines, the people talking. I’d be staring at the same sentence for, like, an hour.” “It doesn’t bother me,” she lied. “You sure?” he asked. She raised her eyebrows, cocked her head, and took a breath, refusing to double down on the lie, but refusing to admit he was right. “Still, though,” he said, “I mean, a library. You could go to a Barnes & Noble—if those even still exist. Is this really the best place to read?” Abina avoided eye contact and leveled the book, as if she could align its edges with the circular table. “If you had to be honest with yourself, why do you really come here?” he asked. Abina drew another breath. “If I had to be honest, I probably come here for the same reason as you,” she said. “To bone strangers?” “Ew. No. That’s gross.” “Aw. Come on. You said you wouldn’t judge me.” “I never said that. Don’t put that on me, Ricky Bobby.” “Come on,” Will said, “I confided to you about my very real but unbelievable magical chair.” Abina averted eye contact. “I’m usually here to meet someone,” Abina said, “But I don’t know who that someone is. I’m always hoping that the next person that sits down next to me will end up being the– “Ha!” Will said. “Told you. Look.” Abina saw that the couple were now engaged in a public display of affection that made everybody around them uncomfortable. They gnawed and clawed at each other as they tumbled toward the exit, knocking over tables and spilling coffee. “Magic! Like I said!” Will stretched out his arm toward her, “5 hunnit. Right here. And you better put my money in my hand.” Abina just stared at him as he continued his gloat. “Man,” Will said, getting up, “How does it feel to have your world flipped upside down? To know that there is something bigger than you in the universe? And more importantly. How does it feel to be so wrong?” While he basked in his self-proclaimed glory, a barista swooped in to clear and clean the empty table. He didn’t see her, but he noticed Abina’s smug delight. His head jerked back to see another guy sit down in his magical chair. “Mm-hm. See,” Abina said, “That’s what you get.” Will slumped back into his seat, defeated, and took a bite of his oatmeal cookie. He scrunched his face and looked at it. “You’ll get ’em next time, champ,” Abina said, coolly leaning back and lifting her book. “That guy doesn’t have a drink yet. He probably just ordered. If you’re lucky, he just ordered a coffee, and he’ll be gone in a minute. But if he gets a double-whip grande, half-caf, triple-shot, sugar-free vanilla, extra-hot, half half-and-half, half oat milk latte with one pump of hazelnut, light foam on the bottom, then it might take a little longer. Either way, soon you’ll be free to go have your nasty, meaningless, disgusting, sloppy sex.” “Okay, Miss Judgy McJudgerson,” Will said. “No judgment,” she murmured into her book. “I just think that kind of instant gratification is base—not to mention unsafe and immature.” Will laughed the loudest he had since he’d sat down. “Really,” he said, “No judgment, huh?” “You don’t like to work for things, do you?” she said, lowering the open book to the table. “Which is surprising, because—” Abina looked at Will as if she could see through his shirt. “Oh. It’s okay when you do it, huh?” “What,” she said, shaking her head, “No. I wasn’t– You– The way you looked was different.” “I’m sure it was.” “Anyway. As I was saying… I bet you're the type of person who runs when things get tough.” “That’s the type of person you think I am?” “Yup. That’s why I said ‘I bet you're the type of person who runs when things get tough.” Across the way, a woman entered the scene. She placed a hand on the back of a chair and addressed the man at the table. He motioned for her to sit. “I’m not the love ‘em and leave ‘em type. Well, I wasn’t.” “You used to be the love ‘em type?” “Kinda. But now I don’t know if I believe in love.” “That’s not a surprise. From what I know about you, it fits.” “I think I’m just a realist,” Will said. “That seems convenient,” Abina said, looking over her shoulder. She looked back, “You sure you’re not a realist because it works with this new lifestyle?” “Maybe,” Will said, “Maybe I’m a little cynical.” “That’s a slippery slope,” Abina accused, “You can use it to justify being selfish.” The couple at the other table was already deep in conversation. The woman smiled with delight– the man’s expression filled with interest. “Look at those two,” Will said. “Everybody’s been those two—smiling, hanging on every word, considerate, excited, playful.” As if on cue, the woman’s mouth dropped, and she lightly smacked the man’s hand, as though he’d just said something cheeky. “And then the smiles fade,” Will continued. “People get used to each other. They grow unresponsive. They start chasing selfish wants and needs. Everything dulls. Things get serious, business-like.” “Well, fun only lasts until it doesn’t,” Abina said. “And then you’re stuck with each other. That’s when you put in the work. You don’t stop building a house just because it’s not as exciting as the foundation. You dig deep and remember why you wanted to build in the first place. That’s love.” “That’s love? So love has to be hard?” “You know the old saying. ‘If you love who you're with, you’ll never work a day in your life.’” “If you love who you're with. What if you just want to get married?” “Are you proposing?” “You eventually want to get married, right?” “You are proposing.” “Or do you want to find someone to marry?” “The difference?” “A friend of mine says some women want marriage so badly they don’t care who it’s with. It’s like they buy the wedding cake first with the plastic bride on top, and the groom is just a faceless placeholder. Marriage is the goal. The man doesn’t matter.” “Lemme guess. This ‘friend’ is single?” Will’s expression all but confirmed the astute observation. “This friend is really you, isn’t it?” “No. I hate being alone. It was kinda forced on me when my wife left.” “You were married? How long ago?” “About five years ago. Well, we got a divorce about five years ago.” “What happened?” “I don’t know,” Will squinted as if trying to pinpoint when everything went downhill. “I think everything changed when I came home one day, and she was dressed to go out. I thought we were gonna get some dinner somewhere, but she left by herself. And then she kept doing that. She started going out all the time. She was married, but she wanted to live the single life. After a while, it was just like I was her roommate.” “Did you talk to her about it?” “Oh yeah. We went to therapy, and everything. Turns out, I was being too controlling. Because I didn’t want my wife going out to clubs and showing her tits to random dudes.” Abina looked at him, confused. “Oh yeah. I found out she posed nude for a photographer.” “Damn.” “She really, really, really wanted to get married. We got married, and then she decided she really, really, really didn’t want to be married anymore.” “That sucks,” Abina said. “I know,” Will agreed, “So, yeah. I don’t know if I believe in love anymore. I don’t know if I believe in marriage.” “Still, though,” she said. “You can’t have an experiment with just one test subject.” “What?” he asked. “What you went through sucks, no doubt, but you can’t use that one situation as an excuse to stop believing in something.” “That ‘one situation’? It was fifteen years of my life. And it hasn’t stopped. I’m paying child support to a woman who turned my kids against me. She stole my identity. I was a father and a husband. That’s who I was. Now… I don’t even know who I am.” “Fifteen years is a long time,” she said. “Twenty is longer.” “You were married for twenty years?” Will asked. “No,” Abina said, “I’m just saying it’s longer. It’s a bigger number.” Will almost smiled. "I was married five years," Abina said. "He was a dick. Conniving, manipulating, and emotionally abusive. But it still hurt when he left.” Abina looked away as if reliving the memory. “Exactly,” Will said, “No such thing as love.” “That’s not true.” “Really? After all that, you still believe? Why?” “Because I was in love. I know what I felt. I can’t just act like it never happened. If I worked a job for 15 years and got fired, I can’t just say, ‘Well, I guess I never really had that job.’ Yes, I did. It was real. It existed.” She shook her head and looked up, trying to find words. “Love isn’t all hearts and rainbows. It’s spades and thunder, too. It’s all-encompassing.” “Spades?” Will asked. “I don’t know, man,” she said, “I was trying to think of the opposite of a heart. I was picturing cards, and the spades look like upside-down black hearts. I don’t know, man, leave me alone.” Will and Abina looked at each other. The seriousness between them melted into a few ridiculous nose chuckles. “You ever watch Seinfeld?” Abina asked. “Oh yeah,” Will said, “Definitely.” “Did you know Elaine didn’t show up in the pilot?” “I don't think that's right.” “I’m tellin’ you. She doesn't come in until episode two… And Cosmo Kramer was originally a shut-in who never left the building.” “Wow. You really know your Seinfeld.” “I was just watching it the other day. The point is, the show had, like, a million episodes. So it's easy to forget things that happened in the beginning. Unfortunately, in life, even if there are only a few bad days, they overshadow the million good ones.” “I feel like you're trying to make a point.” "Close your eyes," she said. "Picture your ex-wife, your kids, fifteen years of marriage. The arguments, the make-ups, movie nights, dirty diapers. The proposal, the stress, the first night, all the time spent—and the time away. Everything you built together." Will’s eyes closed, his eyes darting back and forth beneath his eyelids. "Now, you don’t have to answer out loud. Don’t be defensive. Pretend I’m not here. Just think. Honestly—would you do it all again?" His lips curled slightly. "Open your eyes," she said. "Your seat’s ready." Will looked up just in time to see the chaos left by the second ravenous couple. Chairs were knocked over, a pastry had been stomped into the ground, and a patron was helping another off the floor. “You better hurry this time,” Abina said. Will got up quickly. “Okay,” he said, “It was good–” “Yeah, you too,” Abina said, letting her smile fade as she lifted her book. She reread the same sentence several times, her eyes catching Will’s blurry figure paused ahead. It was as if he stood caught between a hollow life and one of substance, between insensitivity and vulnerability. She looked up and reached for her tea to see that Will was gone. The coffee shop had grown crowded. She lifted the cup to her lips—and found it empty. “Anybody sitting here?” a voice asked. Abina looked up to see another man with his hand on Will’s– on the chair across from her. “Um,” she said, looking over his shoulder, “There was, but I think he left.” He sat. “So,” he asked, “What are you reading?” She lifted the book so he could see the title. “Oh,” he said, “Any good?” “It was,” she said, “But just when I thought the story was about to end, it veered off in a different direction.” “Oh,” he said, “I hate when stories do that. But you probably could tell that there was more. I mean, look how many pages you’ve got left.” Abina looked down at the thickness of the unread portion of the book. “That’s true,” she laughed. “You didn’t save my seat?” Will asked. Abina looked up. For a millisecond, her eyes and mouth widened, then she forced her face back toward neutrality. “I didn’t know you were coming back,” she said. “Yeah, well,” he said, “I didn’t know I was either.” “I guess there was somebody already sitting in your—“ Abina stretched her neck to see that the table with the magic chair was still empty. She looked at Will. The man also looked at Will and then looked at Abina as they stared at each other like he wasn’t there. “Still eating that unhealthy cookie, huh?” Abina asked. “Nah,” Will said, looking around. His eyes landed on a trash can, where he threw the pastry away. Abina smiled. “Do you mind?” she asked the new new stranger. “Not at all,” he said, getting up. When he left, Will placed his hands on the back of his seat. “What were you about to say?” Will asked. “I wasn’t about to say anything,” Abina said. “Before. I asked you why you came here, and you started to say something.” “Did I?” “You said you come here every day hoping that the next person who sits in this chair is… what?” “Oh, you heard that?” “I did.” “Because you pretended like you didn’t hear me.” “I know. It felt like it was getting too real. Someone once told me I run when things get tough. I’m working on it.” Will took a seat. “What about your magic chair?” Abina asked. “I don’t know,” Will said, staring deep into Abina’s soul, “This chair here seems pretty damn magical.”
